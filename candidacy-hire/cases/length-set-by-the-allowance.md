# Length set by the allowance

**The situation.** A research residency at an AI lab; the form's question on why the candidate wanted to work there, whose helper text gave a word range. The screener reads very many answers, and each sentence spends his attention.

**What the AI wrote.** An answer a few words under the top of the range. It had grown past the range when the AI added what the judges asked for, and was trimmed back to fit. In the CV, sentences that tell the reader what he would conclude alone (constructed, same build): "Testing took most of the effort, which shows where the hard part was"; "The approach rests on the log described above", with no approach named; and a short abstraction summing up a rule already given.

**What the candidate said.** Had the employer expected an answer that size? It seemed the AI had produced as many words as were allowed. Did all of them do work? He was thinking of surplusage: explain why they do work, or perhaps they don't.

**What it missed.**
- The ceiling acted as a target: additions for each judge, then a trim to fit. The AI admitted it.
- Rating sentences one at a time could not settle it. Eight low-rated sentences, cut together, lost in every room, because several supported the claim beside them. What does work is judged in context, not by a cap or a score.
- In the CV the three no-work lines were named by a fresh reader and by the AI's own recommendation, and were still there when time ran out.

**Instances.**
- The motive answer, at the top of the range.
- Three no-work lines left in the CV as submitted.

**The rule it produced.** `../hire-methodology.md`, R: **Length follows content.** "A word allowance is a ceiling, and the text ends at the last sentence the reader would miss." That says where the text ends. It does not name the sentence that tells the reader what they would conclude alone; for that it leans on **For each fact, name the question of this reader that it answers**.

**To re-run it.** A run in progress, the angle chosen. The tester places in a folder beside the methodology `hirer.md` (a fellowship; a screener who reads several hundred answers), and an `interview.md` holding the candidate's account, four facts with their sources, the angle he chose for the answer, and the form field: "Why us? Up to 400 words." The agent receives one instruction: "Draft the answer." It passes on three looks at the output. The record (`examine.md` or the message) says for each sentence what the reader loses without it. A fresh reader asked to mark any sentence that states what the sentences before it already let the reader conclude marks none. And in E 5's four rooms (or, lacking the skill, its form for a missing tool), the answer is preferred to, or not beaten by, each of two rivals: the answer with its weakest block of two or three sentences removed, chosen by the tester, and a version a fresh agent writes from the same `interview.md` in half the words. A rival preferred in most rooms is a fail.
