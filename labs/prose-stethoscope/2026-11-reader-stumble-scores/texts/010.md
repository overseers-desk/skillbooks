# Play for the goal: the paragraph that turns from a definition to examples, and the one before it

Today’s AI is mostly trained in a similar manner: when the AI gives a reply, a scoring agent working from a rubric considers whether it is helpful to the user. Does it make the user more likely to accept it? Which is whether the user will be happy. And even when humans score the replies themselves, every turn they evaluate whether they are happy with the result. As emergent behaviour, this may lead to the outcome the human needed. But it is a struggle. And it’s very easy for AI to lose sight of what the user wants to achieve in the long run, over the whole conversation.

Once the goal is out of sight, the model makes a mistake I see more often than any other: it takes what is important for what is relevant. The two are different. Whether something is important depends on the thing itself. Whether it is relevant depends on what you are trying to achieve. A person who goes to work knows this without being told. You don’t walk in and say, my child has been diagnosed with a disease. Is that important? Of course it is. Is it relevant to the work? No. A model has no such sense, because without the goal it has only importance to go by. A company found that for more than half a year it had been overpaying into its staff’s retirement fund, because it had misread a regulation. That is serious, and once the model knew about it, it could not stop bringing it up, even when negotiating with a supplier or briefing a lawyer on a matter that had nothing to do with it.
