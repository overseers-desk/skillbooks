# Play for the goal: the section from the footballer to the education paragraph, before the surplusage passage
Throughout the years, I have developed two ways to explain how a rubric turn-based optimizer would fall short. One is this: suppose that you choose a footballer for a team. You can count how many goals the footballer achieved in a number of seasons or how many goals were made possible thanks to the participation of this footballer. However, suppose you don’t do this but instead you break down a football match into 5-second clips and judge each 5-second clip on whether the footballer has done something that is conducive to a goal, or to his teammates achieving a goal. Every clip scores the same, so the player who does something in the most clips wins, and the one decisive pass counts once. Then, using this 5-second clip method, surely we will be getting the footballer who is busiest: attentive all the time, making more decisions in any given time interval than any of his teammates, rather than those who make the correct decisions and act at the right moment to get the goal.

Another analogy I make more closely resembles how user and agent interact. Suppose we have two pilots. One pilot is a human who can look outside the window but has no access to instruments or any of the steering capability. The only way he can control the plane is to signal to the co-pilot if he is happy or not happy with what’s going on. And the co-pilot is an AI that controls the airplane: the rudder, the throttle, the thrust. The co-pilot has no view of the landscape or of where they are heading. He can change direction, altitude or angle until the human pilot is happy. Are they able to fly to the destination?

Today’s AI is mostly trained in a similar manner: when the AI gives a reply, a scoring agent working from a rubric considers whether it is helpful to the user. Does it make the user more likely to accept it? Which is whether the user will be happy. And even when humans score the replies themselves, every turn they evaluate whether they are happy with the result. As emergent behaviour, this may lead to the outcome the human needed. But it is a struggle. And it’s very easy for AI to lose sight of what the user wants to achieve in the long run, over the whole conversation.

Having talked much about how turn-based training falls short in achieving real-life goals, I do not mean to say that relying on emergent behaviour is not the way to achieve general intelligence. In fact, for a large part of human education, children were graded on micro-improvements one step at a time, similar to the turn-based method used to train AI. The idea is that the student who always had As in primary school will probably always have As in high school, will probably also have a lot of As in university, and will achieve a lot in their lifetime. And very often it is the case. Many of the best lawyers and doctors have been A students since they started school. However, there are two big differences.
