# Play for the goal: the opening, up to the pillar sentence

I have good memories of my kindergarten in China, and of the stories our teacher told us there. One of them I still remember is *The kitten who goes fishing*.

The kitten goes to the river with its mother. Every time a dragonfly or a butterfly passes, it drops the rod and runs after it, while its mother sits by the water and catches fish after fish, until the kitten learns to sit still. The teacher's lesson was that if you cannot stay with what you set out to do, you will achieve nothing.

It is a lesson for a lifetime, though many children forget it soon enough. But it is not a lesson that AI has taken to heart, even though any model can tell you what the story means. An LLM trained to do well at every reply is optimised the way the kitten is, one moment at a time, always going for what it is most likely to catch right then. It works up to a point, since each reply catches something useful. But the bucket stays empty. In the Introduction chapter, I mentioned a case how AI and a human would handle a situation differently. When sorting a customer complaint, and refering to how Bob did it, human asked Who's bob? AI would start a crusde to find him, his employement record etc, while a human would simply he was quite a few years before you - all you need is he is not current - and proceed with the complaint handling, the goal at hand.

A frequent example: after an introduction at a meeting, the model drafted the follow-up email. It wrote out our address, the road name and the year the mill was built, and never said why we wanted to meet.

Sometimes the butterfly is a puzzle. A prospective customer replied to one of our emails, and the model noticed that the email gave two different numbers for the same thing. It spent the rest of the session working out why, and the customer, who was the reason for the email, was forgotten. Asked why, the model said the numbers did not match, so it checked them.

The first pillar is to play for the goal.
