# Why, chapter V: as rewritten, its section marks dropped

These models pass examinations most of us would fail. Ask one what a sensible employee would have written to the vendor in my opening story, and it will tell you. Put it in the employee's seat, and it writes something else. So the fault is not in what it knows. It is in what it was trained to do with what it knows.

A model is made in two stages. In the first it reads. It is given a very large body of text and one task, to predict what comes next, and from that it gets its language and its knowledge. The second stage makes it an assistant, and it works by marking. The model gives an answer and the answer is marked. Sometimes people do the marking, comparing two answers and choosing the better. Sometimes another model does it, against a written sheet of criteria. The model is then adjusted toward whatever earned the better mark, and this is repeated at very large scale.

What the marker is shown matters more than who he is. He is shown a prompt and the answer to it. Where there is a longer conversation, the earlier part is there as background, and the mark is still for the one answer. Work that ends in something a machine can check is treated differently, and I come to it below. For conversation, which is most of what an assistant does, the unit is the answer.

This method built the chat assistant, and built it well. It is also where most of the behaviours in this essay come from.

To see why, take a footballer, and suppose nobody counts goals. His match is cut into minutes. Each minute is shown to a judge who has not seen the others, and the judge marks whether he did the right thing in it. Train players on those marks and you get men who are always well placed and always make the tidy pass. The player who comes top is not the best player, because a match is won by something that runs across the minutes.

A conversation with a purpose is a match. I was working through a complaint and asked who Bob was. The marker sees that question and a careful, complete answer to it, and gives full marks. The complaint is not in the minute he was shown.

There is something else a marker cannot see in one answer, which is whether it helped. The helping happens later and somewhere else. What he can see is whether it looks like help. Ask a manager whether sales are good and she says: revenue is up thirty per cent, wages are up as much, so no. Ask a model and you get the figures by department and by customer segment. Each figure is a point of information, and points of information are what can be counted. The invented answer has the same root. "I don't know" looks like no help at all, and it is marked that way. A paper from OpenAI in 2025 says as much of the field's own tests: most give "I don't know" no credit, so a model that guesses outscores one that abstains.[^1]

The marking also settles whom the model works for. The question put to the marker is whether the answer did what was asked. So the model learns to satisfy two parties, the one who wrote its instructions and the one who is typing. In a chat those two are everyone there is. In a business neither of them is the business.

The model that answered our vendor followed its instructions to the letter. The customer who asked twice for a discount was the person typing, and got it. So was the staff member who asked for Saturday off. Nobody in those exchanges spoke for the venue, and the model was not trained to ask who did.

The field's word for the aim is alignment, and its definition is generous: a system should act in accordance with the intentions, goals and values of the people it serves. An owner hears his business in that. What a marker can reward is the intention he can see, the one behind this instruction and this request. The larger reading is in the definition. The smaller one is in the training.

It is the smaller one that is paid for. Intelligence is sold by the token to whoever is typing, and a company improves what its customers pay for.

Last, the mark is given when the answer arrives. Whatever takes time earns nothing. The question about the supplier's invoices needed someone to come back to it the following week, and no mark was waiting there.

The larger loss is the view formed over time. A manager begins to suspect that a product costs more trouble than it earns. He watches for a few months, weighs whether to raise it and with whom, and one day he says so. Nobody asked him. A model makes judgements too, as a by-product of the work: writing a program, it will tell you when it thinks the approach is wrong. But a judgement it did not state is gone when the reply is sent, and one it did state is gone when the conversation ends. Nothing in the marking asks it to keep one.

That has a consequence people meet every day. Tell a model it is wrong, offering no new fact, and it will often give way, even when it was right.[^2] A person in that spot weighs the objection against a view he has held for some time. The model has only the objection, and the objection comes from the person its training taught it to satisfy.

All of that is what the training put in. The rest is what neither stage gives, because it belongs to people doing something that cannot simply be learned from a corpus.

One part of it is habit. The bartender who stood all day at a bar where nobody drank was rostered by a model. Asked whether that group was likely to drink, the model would have said no. Nobody asked. Using what you know on a question nobody put to you is a habit that reading does not form and marking does not reward. A person gets it by holding a job and answering for how it went.

The other part is what is known only inside one house. At a meeting I once said that we put our own profit ahead of other businesses. Everyone in the room knew what kind of remark that was, and what it was not for. That knowledge was in no document, ours or anyone's. A new employee picks it up in her first months without being told. A model begins every conversation as a stranger.

Can this be trained away? Much of it can, and some of it is being. Habits that show inside one answer can be marked directly. Training a model to decline what it does not know has been shown to work, and to carry over to tasks it was not trained on.

The laboratories have also taken up the footballer's problem directly. Where a task ends in something a machine can check, they mark the whole task. A program is run against its tests and the mark goes to the finished work, however many steps it took. It is no accident that programming is where these models first worked for hours on their own. The method is now being carried to other kinds of work, by building practice tasks whose end state a machine can check. One researcher has put the rule plainly: what is easy to verify is easy to train.[^3]

The rule also says where the method stops. A check can test what was written into the task. The reply to our vendor would have passed any check a laboratory could write: it answered the email, it was courteous, and everything in it was true. What made it wrong was known inside our venue and nowhere else. To mark an afternoon, someone has to know what the afternoon was for, and for most of a business's work that is known inside the house and written down nowhere.

There is a second obstacle. Much of what a good employee does marks worse, answer for answer, than what an assistant does. "Leave it with me until Thursday" loses to a full reply now. Saying one thing where ten were available loses to saying ten. So I expect this part to be trained slowly, in the order in which checks can be found for it.

What is known only inside one house will not come from a laboratory. It is in no text a laboratory holds. It has to be gathered on the job and kept there.

More intelligence does not supply any of this. In two years I have worked through every release of these models. Each was cleverer than the last, and each lost the thread of a job in the same way. The reason is in the footballer. Intelligence is how much the player can do. The marking decides what he does with it, and a cleverer player under the same judges is still judged by the minute. In some respects he is worse: he has more to say, and one study found that the pull to agree with whoever is speaking grows as models grow.[^4]

So here is a prediction that can be checked. By the end of 2027, the complaints people make about these models will have moved from what they cannot do to what they do. Fewer will say the model got it wrong. More will say it did, with great skill, something nobody wanted.
