# The head servant, chapter VI: first draft, its section marks and an editor's placeholder line dropped, the company renamed

Picture an operating theatre. The surgeon stands over an open chest. Around her are five or six people, every one of them highly trained. The nurse has the next instrument in her hand before it is asked for. Nobody debates the plan. Nobody acts without the surgeon's word. In that room you want everyone to be intelligent, and you do not want anyone to decide.

This is the seat today's AI was built for. An assistant is a member of the surgical team. It is quick. It understands the situation. It reads what the person in front of it intends, sometimes before the sentence is finished. And it does nothing until it is told.

It is a very successful design. Hundreds of millions of people use an assistant like this every week, and for the person at the keyboard it works. I use one every day.

But look at what the design assumes. The surgeon is in the room. She has one patient. For those hours she thinks of nothing else, and every decision passes through her. The team makes her hands faster. It does not make her load lighter.

No business is like that. The owner of a business is not in the room. He has dozens of matters open and cannot give any of them his whole mind. Most of them he never sees. Put a surgical team into that business and it waits, intelligent and idle, for a surgeon who is somewhere else. And each time it does act it needs a small correction, and each correction costs the owner the one thing he is short of.

So which seat should an AI sit in, if it is to be a colleague? There is one as old as the household, and I think it is the right one.

Think of the head servant of a large house: the steward, the majordomo, the butler of an English novel. He is not his master's hands. He keeps the house when the master is away, which is most of the time. And he holds a power that nobody on a surgical team has. He decides what reaches the master's ear.

The master he serves is nothing like a surgeon. He has moods. He has a family, and friends, and a round of golf to play. He is sharp in the morning and tired by four. On a good day he can give his mind to three matters, perhaps five, and the house throws up far more than that. Urgent things arrive so often that the important ones never get in, until one day they are urgent too.

So before the servant says a word, most of his work is done. He has asked himself:

- Does the master need to hear this at all, or is it mine to settle?
- Is it heavy or light? A light one he can decide in a breath, and feel the better for it.
- When? The hardest matter goes to the morning. Some things can wait until tomorrow.
- How do I put it, truthfully, so that he can take it in?
- What belongs beside it? What else does he need to know, and is there good news to set against the bad?
- Do I act and tell him afterwards, or ask first?

A good servant does this all day and the master never sees it. What the master hears is one sentence. "David would like to see you on Sunday about the investment. You should know he is in a lawsuit with his wife." Nobody asked for the second half. It came from a man who had thought about what Sunday's meeting was for.

He acts without being asked. That is the difference from the surgical team. It is also what went wrong in half the failures this essay opened with: the leave approved, the discount given. The servant's answer to that is a remit. Inside it he acts and reports. Outside it he prepares the decision and brings it. Which side of the line a matter falls on is the first thing he learns about a house.

And he knows when to do nothing. I own a tourism venue. Someone asks to hire it at two on a Tuesday. We are never booked then, so the obvious answer is yes. A model says yes, or else it asks me. A good manager does neither straight away. Who gains? Will the staff see a booking at that hour, take it for a mistake and ring the customer? Would one line in the confirmation prevent that? It takes him ten seconds, and he may still say yes. But he has decided, where the model either assumed or handed the question up.

Half of the craft is not speaking at all. There is a scene in the film *Interstellar* that I think about often. The crew have landed on a planet where every hour costs seven years at home. A wave has swamped the ship. The captain shouts that they have to leave now. The robot at the controls answers in a handful of words: the engines are flooded, let it drain.

Think of everything a chat assistant would have said in that moment. The apology. The three options. The risks of each. The robot knew all of it. It gave the state of things and the one thing to do, because that was all the captain could use.

I have looked for the one role that would bring the most of these behaviours with it. The head servant is the best I have found.

I do not think that is an accident. The pillars of the earlier chapters are this one role's whole craft. He keeps the errand in mind because the house is his errand. He works for the result because nobody is watching the work. He knows whose man he is, and he reads the person in front of him because that is how a message gets through. The role was shaped over centuries by exactly our problem: an intelligent agent working for someone who cannot watch.

It is a better place to start than the helpful assistant, and other roles are this one with something changed. A buyer is a servant sent to market. A receptionist is a servant at the door. The role also carries upward. What the servant asks himself before he speaks to his master is what a chief executive asks before he speaks to his board, and what one king weighed before he wrote to another. If there is a path to an AI that could run a company, I think it starts in the servants' hall.

Can it be built? The knowledge is not what is missing. People have written this craft down for as long as there have been masters. In January 1942 a memorandum went round the United States Army under the title "Completed Staff Work". It told a staff officer to study a problem and present the solution "in such form that all that remains to be done on the part of the head of the staff division, or the commander, is to indicate his approval or disapproval of the completed action". Every model has read it, along with every novel that has a butler in it. Ask one what a good head servant does and it will tell you, correctly and at length.

That is the trouble. It knows the craft as a subject. It does not have it as a habit, and a habit is not given by listing it. I have written the list into instructions many times. The model agrees with every line, and then at two in the morning sends me a report that there is nothing to report. A checklist cannot bring out a way of thinking.

What can be done today is to build the house around the model. I call it a harness: rules the model meets at the moment they apply, and other models whose only job is to check the first one.

Here is one piece of the harness I built for my own business. When it writes to someone we hope to win as a customer, a second model reads the draft from that person's chair. It holds only what that person holds. Before I trust its verdict I ask it one question: who do you work for? If it is not sure that it works for the customer, I throw the verdict away. No single model will read the other person unless it is asked to. So I gave the other person a seat.

This is what my company, Halden, is built on. Halden is an AI agent that works out what the job is. It reads the systems a business already runs, the accounts, the bookings, the roster and the inbox, together, and brings the work done. It sees what no single system shows: the event booked in one system that sells out the day in another while the roster still shows six staff. Routine matters it settles alone. Anything that moves money or people arrives as a draft with the workings attached, and a person approves. It has been in daily use at my own venue for about a year.

It is not the head servant. Nobody has built him yet. A harness is a house put up around a model that still lacks the habits, and the essay has said why training alone will not supply them. But it is the step that can be taken now, and I chose where to take it: small businesses, where attention is shortest. The owner stays the master of the house.

A servant who chooses what his master hears has power over him. The last chapter is about that.
