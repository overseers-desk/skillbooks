# The wall that bellied, told from below

I am not an expert on why dry-stone walls fail, and people who have studied it properly would know better than I do. In my second year as a waller I built forty metres along the top of a hill farm. After one wet winter a stretch of it bellied out like a full sack.

I rebuilt the stretch with heavier face stones, and the next spring it bellied again. An older waller who was passing pulled a single face stone out of it and put his arm into the wall up to the elbow. There was nothing behind the stone. Thinking about that hollow afterwards, I started to wonder whether the middle of a wall might matter more than its faces, and the interesting thing I found is that a wall seems to fail from the inside.

Suppose the small stones in the middle, which I believe is called the hearting, are thrown in loose. They would presumably settle, and the faces might then have nothing to lean on. From what I have read since, each stone should probably be placed and wedged by hand, and I gather that a long stone is meant to run through the wall every metre or so. I have built that way for twenty years, and so far none of those walls has moved, though I could not say for certain why.
