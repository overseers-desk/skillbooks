# Roadmap: what selling has to teach HIRE, and what has been tested

A job application is a large sale to a committee, made in reply to a published requirement, with one unit to sell and the seller as the product. The lessons below are taken from the bodies of sales practice that fit that description. Each is something the method could make a writer do. A round of the lab picks some of them, writes each into a version of the method or makes it by hand on pages already written, and measures the pages that come out. Each round's worklog names the items it tested by the letters and numbers here.

Terms used throughout: a post is a vacancy with a published advertisement, named by a letter; a page is a candidate's CV written for one post; a writer is a model session that writes pages; a version is one text of the method, and the baseline is the method's text at commit `e51412d3`, where round 1 began; a first draft is a page as a writer first writes it and a finished page is that page after the method's checks; the record is the candidate's file of facts, which every claim on a page has to come from; the holes are the needs the employer states, and the hole step is the method's step that lists them and sets each fact against one; a pair is two pages for one post put before eight judges, who each choose one, judged either on a full read of both or on their opening, the first lines only; a blind batch is one whose writers did not see the version's name.

## How the series is run

The work of a round is to find, by relentless experiment, the best way to bring the candidate an interview. That has two parts. One is getting the judges to prefer one page to another. The other is correcting the judges where they do not behave as a person hiring would. Which route to take to either is for the session running the round to work out. The owner is not expected to supply step-by-step suggestions, and a question about how to run an experiment is settled by running one.

A version is measured by where its pages stand among other candidates' pages. A placement sets one of the candidate's pages in a pile of ten CVs for its post, at the fifth place among nine other people's, before eight judges who each give the order in which they would call the ten to interview. Every page placed for one post meets the same nine rivals. A page's rank is its mean place, from 1 to 10, and a lower rank is better.

Pairs are kept for telling apart two pages written under one text, because there the winner of a pair usually ranks better in the pile. Round 2 took round 1's 104 pairs between two pages that it also placed in the pile, and read the 83 of them that were decided at 6 judges of 8 or more. Of the 20 between pages of one text, the pair's winner ranks better in the pile in 14, worse in 1 and within half a rank in 5. Of the 48 across texts, it ranks better in 11, worse in 8 and within half a rank in 29, and of the 23 of these judged on a full read, better in 2, worse in 6 and within half a rank in 15. The other 15 of the 83 are between pages whose text their file names do not tell. A page merely reworded (L0, below) lost its full read 1 to 7 and its opening 0 to 8 on one base, and its rank on that base moved by less than a tenth.

A batch is sized against the spread between pages of one text in the pile. Round 2 found that pages written under one text differ by about a rank, more than any version or hand change moved a page, apart from LM, the control that removes proof: the twelve pages written under the baseline for post A, first drafts and finished pages together, run from 3.38 to 7.75, a standard deviation of 1.1, where the error of one placement, a mean of eight judges' places, is about 0.3. To see a difference of six tenths of a rank four times in five takes about forty-five pages a text, and to see a whole rank about sixteen, by a one-sided test at 5 per cent.

In pairs, round 1 found that the judges tell two baseline drafts of one post apart about as firmly as they tell a version from the baseline: in 14 of 18 comparisons on the opening of the page and 10 of 18 on a full read. A batch judged in pairs therefore includes comparisons of baseline drafts with each other, and a version's count of comparisons won and lost is reported with the chance of doing as well by chance alone. Three drafts against three baseline drafts can show only a large effect.

A change is tried by hand on a finished page before it is written into the method. An editor, a model session briefed for that one change, makes it on a finished page, the base. A second session checks the edit against the record, and the changed page is judged against its base. Round 2's changes of this kind, L0 to L9 and LM, were each made on two bases, and L9 is L6, L7 and L8 together. That makes the test a screen for what to write next and not a proof. Two of them are controls: L0 rewords the page with nothing changed in what it claims, what it opens with or the order of its parts, and LM makes general every figure and every named system, method, client and backer below the opening. In the pile a change is read against the error of a rank, about three tenths, since two placements of one page can agree more closely than that. A change made this way shows what such a page gains, and whether an instruction makes writers produce the page is a test of its own.

A round records the conditions its writers ran under: the account, the reasoning effort set for the writers' model, the office's instructions loaded or not, and the state of the candidate's record. Round 1 found the effort to be a setting of the profile a writer runs under, different between two accounts on one machine, and not recorded for its first batch.

## The judges, and what they may know

A judge is a fresh session in a sealed room: a new home and settings folder, signed in with a token of an account that is not the office's. It is told it is the person the post reports to and is given only the advertisement and the pages. The owner decided on the sealed room: his name is in the office's plugin and he is the candidate, so a judge on the office's account can learn whose page it is. On the researcher's computer the token is kept at `~/.jail-claude-token`. That file belongs to one computer and is not guaranteed to exist. The owner allows a profile other than a session's own only for a reason stated to him. For the judges he has given it; any other use needs its own.

What keeps a judge in the seat is the decision it is asked to make, which candidate to call to interview. A judge's room is checked from its own record: nothing but the brief note telling it who it is was sent before it chose, no hook ran and no tool was used. Round 1 also put a follow-up question to each judge after its choice, and round 2 in the two batches judged on its first draw of rivals, described below, and in none after. That turn is not clean: it arrived with context added by the office's hooks in 19 of 583 rooms looked at in round 1 and in 13 of the first 40 in round 2. No room had that context before the judge's answer, so the choices stand. The managed settings of the machine the judges run on turn the office's plugins on for every session, a room's safe mode does not override them, and in some rooms they have been installed into the room's own settings folder by the time of a second call. No follow-up question is put to a judge, and nothing is read from one, until a room can be resumed clean.

What round 1 measured of these judges, eight to a comparison with half reading each page first:

- Shown the same page twice, every judge chose the copy it read first.
- Shown a damaged copy of a page, or a page written for another post, they chose the sound page 8 to 0.
- Shown two different drafts, they lean to the page read second. On a full read, 14 of 36 comparisons came out 4 to 4 in that way, each judge naming true differences in favour of the page it had just read.
- They rank and do not grade: more than half of all comparisons come out 8 to 0.

What round 2 measured of the ranking seat, in which a judge is given the advertisement and ten CVs for one post and gives the order in which it would call them, eight judges to a placement:

- Asked only for the three it would call, the judges put a pile in one order every time. Two rivals were called first and second in all 40 rooms, and a third was called third in 37, the candidate's pages in the other 3. A page called fourth and a page called tenth both counted as not called. The seat asks for the whole order.
- With rivals drawn by level and function, three rivals stood well above the candidate's page and the next a rank below it, so it was fourth of ten in nearly every room, and its damaged copy fourth too. Level in the pool's index does not order strength: two of the three rivals called were at director or head level, and four at vice-president or chief level were never called. Rivals are drawn from the pool of other people's CVs kept in the candidate's repository, by ranking the pool against the page the candidate already had and taking the four nearest above it and the five nearest below.
- The same page placed twice ranked 5.38 and 5.50. The error of a mean of eight judges is about three tenths of a rank.
- Placed second and ninth, the same page ranked 5.00 and 5.75, so place moves a page by up to four tenths of a rank, and every page is placed at fifth.
- A page written for another post ranked 9.25, against 5.38 for a page written for this one.
- A copy with its summary replaced by generic sentences over an unchanged body did not fall on either draw of rivals: it ranked 5.00 against 5.44 for the sound page it was made from. A copy with its proof below the top made general fell a rank and a half on both pages it was made on.
- The baseline's finished pages for post A sat mid-pile, between third and eighth: 4.50, 5.88 and 3.88.

The pile's judges are the model that judged the pairs, so the pile is a second method and not a second judge. These properties are worth measuring again whenever the way a judge is run or briefed changes, and for any new seat. Of the four conditions fixed for the ranking seat before its first count, it failed one, the fall of the damaged copy. The four were that the same page placed twice scores within 0.75 of itself, that the damaged copy ranks below the sound page, that a page written for another post ranks below one written for this post, and that the baseline's finished pages sit mid-pile. The control that suits it is the copy with proof made general, which removes what a reader could test.

## What the results so far suggest for the order of work

The owner's steer: "the direction that already showed signs of improvement should be explored first." Among other candidates' pages one change shows it: opening the page on the largest thing the candidate led, which, made by hand, bettered both bases by six tenths of a rank. It was read from what the best-placed pages share, together with two other choices: the trade of an unrelated recent job kept out, and a summary that argues only for this post. The hole step, in the method since the end of round 1, leads in pairs, and its lead is not seen among rivals.

Round 2 offers a reading of this, as speculation. A judge choosing between two pages of one person has only the pages' differences to go on, and decides on them however small. A judge ranking ten people has the people to go on, and the page matters as far as it changes what the judge believes of the person: what was led, what was built, what can be tested in a conversation. That would explain why opening on what was led moves a page among rivals and the grid for the body (L1) does not, though the regrouping wins its full reads in pairs. The spread between pages of one text may be mostly the same thing: which facts of the record a writer happened to put where a reader of ten pages sees them. Whether the hole step puts lower on the page what a reader among rivals looks for first is not shown: its pages state the engineers led early at least as often as the baseline's.

A written batch could test three things.

- An instruction that the page opens on the largest thing the candidate led, keeps the trade of an unrelated job out, and holds the summary to this post (C1, C2). Whether writers follow it can be counted on the page itself, on a few pages a text, without judges. Whether it moves the page among rivals cannot be seen cheaply: with pages of one text a rank apart, an effect of six tenths of a rank needs about forty-five pages a text to be seen four times in five, and a whole rank about sixteen.
- Choosing among several pages. Three pages of one text placed in a pile, with the best one sent, would gain about a rank, for three times the writing and twenty-four judges an application, three placements of eight.
- The record. The four questions it leaves open about the scale of what the candidate led bear on what the pile's judges hold against the page more directly than any wording tried (G2).

Round 1's list of work still to do stands otherwise: the trace of each claim to its source, which the writer of the page does not run (D3); the edited text with its rules repaired (H1); the writer's job defined by the employer's reply (F2); the check that a page speaks in one person, which as worded lets a page wholly in the third person pass; the modules, the reader's questions lifted into files of their own; the writers' conditions as versions, such as reasoning effort and the office's instructions; the candidate file, as a possible source of third-person words and of two readings of one date; other candidates. A2 needs a way to show a page to the two readers separately. G1 and G3 wait for the second stage.

A round costs most in the session that runs it, then in writers, then in judges. Round 2 ran no writer and placed its pages before 872 judges' rooms for 106 dollars as the rooms report it; round 1's writers cost 767 dollars by the same count. A round is sized to end within the session that runs it, the control comes before the versions, and reading goes to subagents, each returning one screen.
