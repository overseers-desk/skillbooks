# Candidate adjudicator

Read `briefs/coder-brief-block.md` first, then the sections of `{{CODEBOOK}}` on what the offering is and what falls in scope, then in `{{AMENDMENT}}` the definitions of the variables your prompt names and the whole of the amendment that states their judgement (the questions a reader answers and the exclusions by name), then the shard file your prompt names under `{{CORPUS}}/candidates/`. You may open a source file a row names, under the unit's folder in `{{PAGES}}` as `{{CORPUS}}/shards/units-with-text.txt` gives it, where the sentence with its neighbours does not settle the question. Nothing else is open to you: no coded table, no other reader's file, no card, no finding.

A script has found every place where a unit's pages print a listed phrase of the variables your prompt names. Each row of your shard is one sentence of one unit, with the sentence before and after, the file, the phrases the script matched and the variable, class or value each belongs to, how often the sentence repeats across the unit's pages, and flags for a sentence near the offering's own word and for a short row that may be a navigation label. Printing a listed phrase states nothing by itself. Your work is the judgement the amendment separates from the vocabulary: whether the sentence states that value of that variable, or addresses that class of buyer, about the offering, by the amendment's questions and with its exclusions applied by name. A word in another sense (a guide who leads a tour where the list means a youth group, a camp site where it means a holiday programme, "free" of charge for something else, a place whose name holds a listed word) states nothing, and a navigation label addresses no one.

Judge every row of `<your shard>-pairings.tsv`, which the orchestrator cut beside your shard, and write one row for each: one pairing is a unit with a variable and a class or value that appears among its matches, and the two readers of a shard judge the same list. Judge one unit at a time, reading all of its rows, and work a few units at a step, appending their rows to your file before you read the next. A reader that reasons through a whole shard in one reply runs past the length a reply may have, and a file written a few units at a time loses only the last step to a cut.

Write `{{CORPUS}}/candidates/adjudicated/<your file name>.tsv`, tab-separated, no quoting, under the header that is the one line of `{{CORPUS}}/candidates/header-adjudication.tsv`, one row for every pairing: the unit's key column, under the name the candidate table's header gives it, which your prompt states; `variable`; `class_or_value`; `stated` (1 where at least one sentence states that value, or addresses that class, about the offering; 0 where none does; 8 where you cannot decide, saying why); `deciding_candidate_id` (the first such sentence in the shard's order, empty on 0); `deciding_sentence`; `reasons_rejected` (the names of the exclusions or reasons that account for the rows you rejected, each once, separated by "; ", with "navigation label" and "word in another sense" available beside the amendment's own); `note` (what a second reader would most likely do differently, or empty). Apply the rule as written even where you think it wrong, and say so in the note. Where the rule has no named answer for a sentence (a heading that names no person over its own text; a hosting or partnership sentence; whether an exclusion written for one variable reaches another), write the note in the coder block's one grammar, `rule point: <section number> <the question in a few words>`, so the orchestrator can gather the points of every reader for the next amendment.

Check before you finish, by script over your file: one row for every row of your pairings file; every row with the header's number of fields; and no kind of a class stated where you coded that class 0, since the merge would otherwise take the kind's code over yours. If you were resumed after a cut, read your own file first and continue from the first unit it does not hold.

Report in under 120 words, with every figure counted by script from the file you wrote and not recalled: pairings judged, how many stated by variable and class, and the rule points you had to guess at.
