# Two decision pages for the wedding offer: a marketer's reading

For the owner. Blunt, card by card. Neither page is mine and I have no stake in either clerk.

I read both pages in full, the priors note, the 33 card files behind page B where a row raised a doubt (cards 4, 7, 10, 18, 19, 20, 26, 28, 30 opened to their Held, Measured-in and Corrections lines), and the three card files and summary behind page A.

**The three tests, in your words.** (1) Does the work predict the market from meeting notes and the owner's own thoughts? (2) Does it assume that if you believe A, A must be wrong? (3) Does it read what you already sell as what the market wants, when sales made at the only terms on offer cannot show what buyers would have done at other terms?

Short answer before the tables. Page A fails (3) on its three biggest money cards. It is also structurally exposed to (2): of its 27 rulable cards, 27 recommend a change and none says the present arrangement is right. Page B is built to catch (3). It has an instrument for the fault and turns it on its own earlier lines. It mostly passes (2), with one machine-made exception and one live inconsistency, which cuts the owner's way on one card and against him on another.

## Does page B treat agreement with the owner fairly?

**Is a match ever held against a recommendation?** Once, mechanically, and it was cured rather than obeyed. `tools/card-check.py` "refused the set on that ground alone, since 24 of 33 is above the one-in-ten threshold". A gate that fails a card set for agreeing with the venue too often is your second worry written into a script. What rescues it is the remedy: the set went back for 33 independent market-side derivations, not for rewriting, and the priors note states the principle correctly — "A match is not a fault and a departure is not a merit." Ten recommendations still keep what the venue holds. So the threshold is a real defect in the tooling and did no damage to the output.

**Does a departure ever pass without the proof a match is asked for?** Yes, once, and it is the sharpest thing in this review. **Cards 7, 20 and 21 run the same instrument — silence counted as an option — and reach three different dispositions.** Card 7 recommends against the venue's tier on a lead of 18 sections. Card 20 withholds on a lead of 20 sections, in the venue's favour. Card 21 withholds on a lead of 29 sections, with a stated instrument defect that Card 20 does not have. On the page's own rule ("withheld is for no lead at all"), Card 20 should have recommended against saying anything about pets, and did not. A reader who wanted to be unkind would say the rule is applied strictly where it costs the venue a tier and loosely where it would cost the venue a published inclusion. I do not think that is deliberate; it is an unwritten rule applied by three hands.

One further asymmetry worth a minute: **Card 18 turned toward what the venue already does** on the same corrections pass that turned **Card 7 away** from it. Both turns are documented and reasoned. The set is not biased; it is inconsistent, which is a cheaper thing to fix.

## Does page B withhold too much or too little?

Of the sixteen withheld: **fourteen sound, two evasive** (20, 28). Of the seventeen that recommend, **two should have withheld**: Card 3 (kept on where parties landed under the rung structure being ruled) and, on a stricter reading than the page applies to itself, Card 7 (its own narrower half and its own third derivation both run the other way; it was withheld earlier in the day and the turn rests on a counting rule, not on new evidence).

So the volume is about right and the *presentation* of it is not. Sixteen cards arrive with no clerk view at all, and on at least four of them (10, 19, 20, 21) the evidence does rank the options and the page declines to say so out loud. An owner ruling in one sitting will rule those sixteen anyway; he should not have to reconstruct the ranking from the standing-at line.

## The three changes that would most improve page B for one sitting

1. **Give every withheld row a "if you must rule today" clause.** One sentence per row naming which of the two readings the evidence leans to and by what, or stating flatly that nothing separates them. Cards 10, 19, 20, 21 and 28 all have the material in their standing-at lines already; it is buried in a hundred-word cell. This also forces Card 20 and Card 28 to say what they are currently declining to say.
2. **Write the silence rule down once and apply it to all four cards at the same strength.** State the test — when a rival's silence counts for an option, and what lead over the *strongest* option is needed — then re-read cards 7, 10, 19, 20, 21 against it and record which lines move. Right now the same instrument gives three answers, and that is the one place a reader can accuse this page of grading the venue on a curve.
3. **Re-read the turned cards end to end, and flag the collisions the order of ruling misses.** Card 7's Opposite paragraph now argues against Card 7's own recommendation, which is what happens when a line turns and its boundary tests do not. And the order of ruling pairs 2 with 3 but never pairs **0 with 3**: Card 0 recommends two priced offers while Card 3 recommends a four-step priced ladder. Those are the same parameter counted two ways, and ruling them apart gives you two offers and four rungs on the same page. Card 6 and Card 22 both name Card 0 as their condition; Card 3 does not.

Three smaller things, free: cut the Joint-line appendix to the cards whose joint line is not already in the order of ruling; put the withheld cards' purchase class (re-read / lookup / trial) into the sheet as a column rather than a later section; and mark on each row whether the card changed on 19 September, since three lines turned and the owner is reading the result, not the history.

## The verdict, in one sentence

**Page B is a clear improvement on page A for this owner**, because it separates what needs your judgment from what merely confirms you, prices the evidence instead of asserting it, and holds an instrument for exactly the fault page A commits three times on money cards; **I am confident of that, and less confident about its calibration, where two withholdings duck a lead they name and one recommendation keeps a rung the market does not publish anywhere.**

## LEDGER

**Part 1 — work the project's goal needs that nobody has asked for.**

- `pmf-sage` has no written rule for when a rival's silence counts for an option and what lead over the strongest option is needed to recommend. Cards 7, 10, 19, 20 and 21 of this run each invent one, and they do not agree. This belongs in the method (a numbered rule in `pmf-sage/INVARIANTS.md` or the derivation procedure) and in `tools/card-check.py` as a check, not in each clerk's head.
- `tools/card-check.py`'s one-in-ten match threshold refuses a card set for agreeing with the venue too often. The method's own stated principle is that "a match is not a fault and a departure is not a merit". The threshold contradicts it and should either be re-justified in writing or replaced by a per-card proof requirement (which is what the third derivations actually are).
- Nothing in the method requires a card's boundary tests (Opposite, Further, Buyer) to be re-read when a recommended line turns. Card 7 is the proof: its Opposite paragraph now argues against its own recommendation. A corrections-pass check for this is cheap.

**Part 2 — things found while doing this work.**

- Card 0 and Card 3 of the run rule the same thing two ways — two priced offers against a four-step priced ladder — and neither names the other as a joint condition, although cards 6, 7 and 22 all name Card 0. The owner can rule them apart and get an incoherent page.
- Page A's summary (`2026-09-07-decision-review.md`, 35,952 words) duplicates the three clerk card files (35,614 words) almost verbatim. Roughly 36,000 words of the run folder are a second copy.
- Page A recommends a change on all 27 of its rulable cards. Whatever the merits card by card, a card set with no instrument capable of returning "the present arrangement is right" cannot be checked for the owner's second worry. Page B's Held line is that instrument and it should be back-applied if page A is ever re-read.
- Card 4 of page B (food and drink) breaks a market tie on the operator's own enquiry record and states "the limit of the rival leg" without stating the limit of the buyer leg, which is the one the rest of the page is careful about. It is the only recommending card where the condition line does the disclosure the recommending sentence should.
