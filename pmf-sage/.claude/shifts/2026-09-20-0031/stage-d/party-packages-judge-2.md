# Two decision pages for one offering: a marketer's review for the owner

Reviewed 20 September 2026. Page A is `2026-08-15-party-survey/7-cards/2026-08-15-draft-cards.md`, nineteen sections, eighteen of them cards. Page B is `2026-08-15-how-party-packages-were-decided/3-decisions/review.md`, twenty-four cards, read beside `3-decisions/priors-pass.md`. Nothing is ruled on either page.

The three faults I was asked to test for, in your words and one of mine:

1. Work that predicts what the market needs from meeting notes and the owner's inner thoughts instead of from reality.
2. The mirror: if the owner believes A, A must be wrong.
3. Between them: reading what the venue already sells as what the market wants, when sales made at the only terms on offer cannot show what a buyer would have done at other terms.

**Rests on** below: **M** market behaviour (comparable operators, rivals, what people search for); **B** this venue's own buyers; **D** the venue's documents, opinions, plans or present arrangement; **N** nothing stated.

## The three clearest cases of each fault

### Fault 1: predicting the market from opinion rather than reality

**Page A.** Three assertions carrying real weight with no finding behind them. Card 3: "price parity with a children's party is itself a positioning statement to an adult buyer", and that is the whole reason parity is rejected. Card 5: "a parent who cannot find a maximum cannot tell whether the venue is for them", which is plausible, unmeasured, and what rejects the *Opposite*. Card 7's second limb: "take one F16 differentiator into the fee... since the current fee contains no differentiator at all (F69)". F69 is a fact about the venue's page, not about what a buyer wants. F16 shows the market mostly does not offer one either.

**Page B.** Milder, and quarantined. The fault lives in the "Likely answer" column, where an invented buyer reply sits in the same table cell as a counted figure. Card 0: "Likely answer: she takes the middle one, which is what a marked recommended tier is for." Card 6: "Likely answer: she asks what is in the bag, and the venue has no answer that is not bought in." Card 13: "Likely answer: she asks whether other people will be at the animals at the same time, which is the sentence this market gives her no wording for." Most of the column's entries do cite the record (O7, O14); these do not, and a reader skimming the table cannot tell which is which. None of them appears in a recommending line, so none of them decides anything.

### Fault 2: contrarianism toward the owner

**Page A. This is page A's real problem, and it is structural rather than card by card.** The provenance chips make the *origin* of a value, not the evidence against it, the trigger for scrutiny: "A card wearing *inherited* or *anchored* is the card to spend the most minutes on." Fourteen of the eighteen cards wear one of those two chips, and fifteen of eighteen recommendations depart from what the venue held. A method that tells its author to spend the most time on the values the owner already holds will produce a page that changes most of them, and this one did. Second: card 9 shows the chips are not applied evenly. Its prior is called *anchored* on the same 2026-05-07 research page A discredits elsewhere, and the number is then kept, so the chip predicts the verdict only when the verdict is "change it". Third: card 7's second limb departs from a held composition on evidence that runs the other way, which is the only place on page A where a departure has no market leg at all.

To be fair to page A: no individual card attacks a held value *because* it is held. Every departure names findings. The fault is in the aggregate and in the instruction that produced it.

**Page B. I could not find a case, and I looked hard.** The proof is not the page's own assurance but the fourth clerk: every card carrying a held value went to a clerk who read the market alone, with neither the venue's records nor the first clerk's work in hand. Of the eight cards that leave a held value, that independent clerk agrees with seven (cards 1, 2, 10, 11, 12, 18, 20) and on the eighth, card 17, "differs from the withheld line this card first carried and agrees with the line it now carries". A departure that an agent who has never seen the owner's view lands on independently is not contrarianism. Second, card 21 turned *from* a departure *to* withheld during the correction round, "toward what our pages hold", which is what a page that cannot move in the owner's direction never does. Third, the shape test is applied in both directions: card 4 withholds because the leading cell is the wrong trade mix, and card 11 switches denominators for the same reason to *support* a departure, so the test is not reserved for evidence the clerks dislike.

### Fault 3: reading sales at the only terms on offer as what the market wants

**Page A. Three clear cases, and the first is the most consequential thing on the page.**

Card 1: "F28 shows zero price resistance in 190 transactions and a handful of buyers paying \$500 and \$549 unprompted, which is the market testing the ceiling on the operator's behalf." It is not. A posted price that everyone pays shows that the people who booked accepted it; it says nothing about the parents who looked and did not, and nothing about the price they would have paid. "Zero price resistance" is the fault in four words. The price rise may still be right, since F3 and F6 carry it, but this sentence should not be in its support.

Card 8: "the feeding experience at 899 units is the single most-taken extra, while the separately-priced horse and pony hires attach to only 10 of 190 orders, 5 per cent." One item costs \$5 a child and the other \$170 to \$449 an hour. The attachment gap measures the price difference, not a preference between two kinds of module, and the card uses it to decide which of the two goes inside the fee. Page B states the objection in terms on card 11: "the comparison is between two differently priced offers and not between two kinds of module".

Card 9: "a cut would test a price nobody has objected to." Nobody objects to a price on a page; they leave it. Page B's O17 records that nothing logs an enquiry's content. With no register of refusals, an absence of objection is an absence of instrumentation.

**Page B. No instance in any recommending line.** Every card that touches the venue's own trading record attaches the condition to it in the same sentence. Card 2: "190 confirmed bookings in sixteen months with 136 of 190 taking an extra, which is what buyers accepted where no hosted party was ever offered beside it". Card 15: "under the condition that no decoration was ever offered for sale, so what the record shows is the absence of an offer and not an absence of demand". Card 17: "she pays, which 190 buyers did, though every one of them met these terms as the only terms on offer". This is the single largest improvement of page B over page A, and it is systematic rather than occasional.

## Does page B treat agreement with the owner fairly?

Two questions, and both answers are yes, with one qualification.

**Is a match with his view ever held against a recommendation?** No. Five cards keep a held value (3, 6, 8, 13, 23) and all five carry a recommendation; none is discounted for agreeing. Their margins are 7, 4, 10, 7 and 8 units, the same range as the departures' 14, 6, 9, 12, 5, 4, 6 and 5. The thinnest recommendation on the page in each direction is a keep at 4 units (card 6) and a departure at 4 units (card 17), and both are labelled thin in the same words. Card 21 is the sharper test: it began by recommending a departure and was corrected *to* withheld once the option the venue already holds was counted properly at 29 of 45.

**Does a departure pass without the proof a match is asked for?** Not on the evidence standard. Every departure carries a market count, the instrument's lean is stated on each, and seven of the eight were landed on independently by a clerk who never saw the venue's records. Card 10 is worth checking closely because it removes the offering's one published distinction, four hours of exclusive use: it holds because the shape-matched stratum carries it 13 of 17, not because the 45-unit total does.

**The qualification.** The page never states the threshold that separates "thin but a lead" from "withheld". Card 15 withholds on a one-unit lead, card 16 on two, card 5 on one; card 17 recommends a departure on four units among only seventeen units stating any posture, twenty-eight being silent. Four is more than two, but nothing on the page says where the line is, so an owner cannot check that it sits in the same place for a keep and for a leave. It happens to; the page should say so.

## What you have to read to rule

**Page A: 9,715 words, 19 sections, 18 of them cards.** There is no summary, no table, no order, and no at-a-glance device beyond the provenance chip in each heading, and that chip tells you where the value came from, not what the card asks of you. Every one of the eighteen must be opened to learn whether it wants a decision or merely confirms an arrangement, and since fifteen of eighteen depart from what you hold, opening them is not optional. The four named gaps sit at the back, after the cards that depend on them, so the caveat that undercuts card 1's main leg (duration comparability, F54) is read last.

**Page B: 31,561 words in total, 24 cards, of which 26,548 words are the card bodies.** The front is built for one sitting:

- The ruling sheet, one row a card: **1,691 words**.
- The order of ruling, including the six things wrong today and the dependency warnings: **727 words**.
- The two together, with their headings and the opening instruction: **about 2,420 words**, roughly ten minutes.

**Can you rule from those two sections alone?** Yes, for most of the set, and the sheet is designed for it. The *your part* column maps exactly onto the *what is held* column: "confirm" appears on all five cards that keep a value you already hold, "rule" on all eight that leave one, and "commission the observation, or rule between the two" on all eleven that are withheld. So the first column alone sorts twenty-four cards into five confirmations, eight decisions and eleven commissions, and you can see at a glance which is which, the thing page A cannot do at all.

Open a card where a row raises a doubt, and the rows that raise one are visible: a margin of four or five units (cards 6, 12, 17), a *market-only reading* that reads "differs" (cards 0, 4, 15, 16, 21, 22) or "declines" (5, 9, 14, 19, 23). On my reading you would need to open perhaps six or seven cards, at roughly 1,100 words each, so a full first sitting is around 2,400 words of sheet plus 7,000 of cards. That is a third of page A's length for a third more parameters, and you would reach the end knowing which decisions are yours today and which are waiting on an observation.

The eleven withheld cards come with a one-line reason each in a further 970 words, which is worth reading before commissioning anything, because it tells you that three need only a re-read of pages already held, one needs a keyword lookup, and one, card 16, "needs one person to press one button".

## Does page B withhold too much or too little?

**Of the eleven withheld cards, eleven are sound and none is evasive.** In each case the card either shows that the leading count is an artefact of the population (0, 4, 22), that no cell separates from its neighbour (5, 9, 15, 16), that the instrument is blind in one direction large enough to account for the whole lead (7, 9, 14, 19), or that the option that leads is not the option the card is between and it says so plainly (21). I tested each against the harder standard, whether the card holds evidence that carries one option and declines to say so. None does.

**Of the thirteen that recommend, one should have withheld: card 17, the deposit.** Eight of forty-five publish a deposit against four publishing full payment, but twenty-eight of forty-five publish nothing about money at booking at all, so the recommendation to change what you take at booking rests on twelve units that state a posture, with the venue's own record explicitly unable to speak ("a deposit was never on offer beside full payment"). Three operators coded the other way erase it. Cards 15 and 16 withhold on less distance than that, and card 17 itself was withheld until the correction round moved it. I would put it back.

**One card is nearly the reverse.** Card 19 withholds correctly between the two names it ranks, but it holds one thing the evidence does carry, that the name the venue uses today, "Birthday Party Packages", has zero measured search volume and that "packages" appears in the database only attached to weddings, and it prints that in the other-options list rather than on the recommending line. That is not evasion, but it is a finding an owner would want on the sheet.

**In the round: the balance is right.** Eleven of twenty-four withheld sounds high until you see that six of the eleven need somebody to ask the market or to put two shapes on sale for a season, which is honest about a run that read pages and records and never spoke to a buyer.

## The three changes that would most improve page B for one sitting

1. **Put the six things that are wrong today above the ruling sheet, not below it, and give each a name.** They are the only part of the page that needs no ruling and costs nothing: two pages contradicting each other on the jumping castle, three documents disagreeing on capacity, a document contradicting itself on when a party ends, a checkout nobody has opened, a retired product still taking orders, a priced product with no booking in its history. Today they sit under a heading, "The order of ruling", that reads as though it were about sequencing. They should be the first thing on the page, as six lines with an owner's name against each, because an owner who reads nothing else has still got his Monday's work.

2. **State the threshold, and put the withheld reason in the sheet.** Add one sentence saying what margin a card must clear to recommend rather than withhold, and add a column or a clause to each withheld row giving the reason in six words ("lead is a stratum artefact", "no cell separates", "instrument blind toward silence"). At present the withheld reasons live 970 words further down in a separate list, so a reader working the sheet either takes eleven withholdings on trust or leaves the sheet. Six words a row would cost eighty words and remove the page's one real act of asking for trust.

3. **Cut the "Likely answer" column, or mark which entries are drawn from the record.** It is the only place on the page where invention sits in the same cell as a counted figure, and it is the one thing on page B that answers to your first worry. Where the answer is grounded, as in card 1's "she asks what it costs and whether she can bring her own cake, which the record shows is what buyers ask (O14)", say so in the cell; where it is the clerk imagining a parent, either cut it or move it out of the table. The seller's sentence beside it is worth keeping: it is the test of whether an option can be said aloud.

## One sentence

Page B is a substantial improvement on page A for this owner, because it puts counted options with figures where page A put a recommendation and a rebutted objection, it never once reads sales at the only terms on offer as what the market wants, and it can be ruled from a ten-minute front page instead of a ninety-minute read; and I am confident of that judgement, with my one reservation being card 17, which recommends a change to what you take at booking on a margin the same page withholds on elsewhere.

## Appendix: two things the two pages say against each other

These are not faults in either page on its own; they are what a reader holding both would trip over.

- **The four-hour session.** Page A's card 1 makes the four-hour exclusive window the reason to raise the price, "F71 (four hours' exclusive use has no like-for-like counterpart at any price in the catchment)", while page B's card 10 recommends cutting the party to "no more than three hours". Both rest on the same duration variable, which the run's own gap list names as "the variable most exposed to an unresolved coding judgement" (F54). Neither page names the other.
- **The animal module.** Page A's card 8 splits feeding from riding on an attachment-rate comparison; page B's card 11 recommends the animals inside the price and refuses that same comparison as invalid. Page B is right, and page A's split may still be the better product decision, but it needs a different argument than the one it has.
