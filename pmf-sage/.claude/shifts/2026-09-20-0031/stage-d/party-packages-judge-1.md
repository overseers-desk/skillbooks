## A note before the tables, because the two verdict counts are not comparable

Page A clears a bar of "an argument that cites a market finding". Page B clears a bar of "a count over a named population that survives its own instrument's lean". Graded each on the evidence it shows, page A collects more EARNED rows than page B. That is an artefact of the bar, not of the work. Every page A card that page B re-counted came back thinner than page A put it, and on eleven parameters page B found it could not rank the options at all where page A had recommended without hesitating. Read the two counts as answering different questions: page A's says how many of its arguments hang together, page B's says how many of its counts separate two options.

## The three clearest cases of each fault

### Fault one: predicting the market from the venue's own notes, plans and opinions

**Page A.**
1. Card 13, the session. The whole case for keeping 9 AM to 1 PM on two days is four internal documents: kitchen hours, the walk-in ride service at §2.3, the 3 PM wedding rule at §2.6. The market leg (F71) says only that nobody else does it. "keeping one session protects the afternoon for the public trade, the walk-in rides and the wedding business, which is a real defence and the reason the shape exists." That is the venue explaining itself to itself.
2. The provenance chips. "**Anchored**: evidence fitted around a pre-held value" and "A card wearing *inherited* or *anchored* is the card to spend the most minutes on." This grades a value by the motive imputed to whoever wrote it down, not by evidence. Cards 1 and 9 both dismiss the 2026-05-07 research's conclusion on that ground: "a judgement fitted around the held price rather than derived to it."
3. Card 9. "the 2026-05-07 research computed a 12-child party at \$698 all-in and pronounced it 'well-pitched' against a competitor scan, which is evidence fitted to the held numbers." The card then holds the same prices anyway, so the demolition does no work except to mark a colleague's opinion as contaminated.

**Page B.** Nearly clean. Every figure is a count over a named file with a stated re-run rule, and internal documents appear only in the capability column, labelled as cost lines. The residue:
1. Card 10's capability line is an operator's interest, "a shorter party is the only way two parties fit a day", printed beside a market count, in a column the owner reads as cost rather than as argument.
2. The "Likely answer" sentences throughout are invented buyer speech. Card 12: "she asks whether that person stays for the cake, which is the question a host is bought to make unnecessary." Useful as a seller's rehearsal, but it is authored, not observed, and it sits in the same table cell as counted figures.
3. Card 19's capability column carries the venue's own search residue ("27 impressions and 3 clicks at best position 7.7") into a card about what the market types. The card marks it: "the operator's own residue points the same way and cannot settle it, being one site's leavings and not the market."

### Fault two: treating the owner's belief as evidence against itself

Neither page contains a single card that departs from a position the owner has taken. He has taken none: all 24 rows read open, and page B says so plainly: "Nothing in this set argues against a decision you have made, because none has been made on any of these parameters yet." **CONTRARIAN count is 0 on both pages.** Three structural notes, in descending order of seriousness.

1. **Page A's chip scheme is a standing tilt.** A held value is marked *inherited* or *anchored* before any evidence is weighed, and the reader is told to spend the most minutes there. The suspicion is procedural, not evidential.
2. **Page A card 4** retires The Grand Gathering partly as "a maintenance liability that also pollutes any count of what the operator sells", a tidiness argument about the venue's own records dressed as a product ruling. The zero-bookings fact carries the card; this sentence does not.
3. **Page B's correction round moved lines both ways and records which.** "Card 17 turned from withheld to a thin recommendation for a deposit, which is away from what we sell today... Card 21 turned the other way, from recommending a named customer's words to withheld... A round that moved lines in one direction only would not be a test, and this one moved them in both." This is the strongest single piece of evidence in either page that fault two is not operating.

### Fault three: reading what the venue already sells as what the market wants

**Page A.** Three instances, all load-bearing.
1. Card 1: "F28 shows zero price resistance in 190 transactions and a handful of buyers paying \$500 and \$549 unprompted, which is the market testing the ceiling on the operator's behalf." One price was on sale. Everybody paid it. That shows nobody refused \$399; it cannot show what \$549 would have taken.
2. Card 7, rejecting a fully inclusive fee: "F30 shows the extras layer is the operator's most reliable revenue behaviour, attaching to nearly three orders in four, and folding it in would raise the headline against F3's band with nothing visible bought." Page B answers this directly on the same parameter: "what 136 of 190 orders show is buyers taking extras that were there, not preferring a ladder to a fee with everything in it."
3. Card 8, deciding which animal module goes inside the fee: "Feeding has no such ceiling: bags are sold year-round at the gift shop, which is exactly why it attaches at 899 units and riding at 5 per cent." Feeding is \$5 a child and a ride is \$170 to \$449 an hour. Page B's card 11 says what that comparison actually is: "the comparison is between two differently priced offers and not between two kinds of module."

**Page B.** Systematically inoculated. Every card that cites the venue's record prints a condition line naming what was on sale beside it, tagged O23, and the ruling sheet's "what is held" column keeps the held value visible without letting it vote. The residue is one defect, and it is a presentation defect rather than a reasoning one.

1. **The sheet's "rests on" column says "both" on eight rows where the card's own text voids the buyer leg.** Card 13 is the sharpest: the recommending line says "the operator's own record points the same way, 190 confirmed bookings against pages stating exclusive hire of a named space throughout, though the condition is that no other arrangement was ever offered beside it, so the record carries this option against nothing." The card says the leg carries nothing; the sheet prints "both". Same pattern on cards 3, 6, 8, 11, 18 and 23. An owner reading the sheet and not the card will believe his own buyers have voted on eight parameters where, by the page's own account, they have not.
2. Card 23: "190 confirmed bookings spread across sixteen event months with no month below three" is offered as the own side agreeing, under a condition line, on a card whose demand instrument does not exist.
3. Card 3: "105 of 105 confirmed bookings on the cheaper product charged the listed price". Correct as a fact about publication discipline, cited as if it were a fact about buyers' preference for a published number, then corrected two clauses later.

## Does page B treat agreement with the owner fairly?

**Is a match with his view ever held against a recommendation?** No. The clearest test is card 21, which turned during the correction round *toward* what the venue already does (no testimonials) once silence was counted as the option it is at 29 of 45. A page running fault two would not have allowed that turn. Keeps are not asked for more than leaves: card 6 keeps a value and is reported thin at a 4-unit margin; card 12 leaves one and is reported thin at a 5-unit margin. The same instrument-lean paragraph is written on both.

**Does a departure ever pass without the proof a match is asked for?** Twice, and they are the two cards to open.

1. **Card 2.** It departs on nine paid-entry indoor activity venues while card 4 refuses to rank the same catchment for the identical reason and card 11 formally switches stratum to escape it. The page states the rule and then does not apply it on the one card where applying it would have put the venue's present shape in front.

## What the owner has to read in order to rule

**Page A: 9,715 words, 18 cards, no glance layer at all.** There is no summary table, no ordering, no mark distinguishing a card that confirms the present arrangement from one that overturns it, and no statement of how many cards there are until the closing paragraph. The eighteen cards are identical in shape and length, so a card recommending a \$150 price rise across the whole book looks exactly like a card recommending a FAQ partial. The only triage offered is the provenance chip, which sorts by suspicion rather than by consequence. He must read all 9,715 words, and after reading them he still has no order in which to take the decisions and no list of what he is actually being asked to decide.

**Page B: 31,561 words, 24 cards.** The front matter is 5,013 words; the cards are 26,548.

**Could he rule from the ruling sheet and the order of ruling alone?** Largely yes, and that is the page's real achievement. The sheet is 1,768 words, the order of ruling 1,072, which is **2,840 words, about twelve minutes**. It carries: a *your part* column that sorts the 24 rows into 5 confirms, 8 rules and 11 commission-or-choose; the margin each recommendation stands at; whether the recommendation keeps or leaves what the venue holds; an independent clerk's landing; and an order that names which card silently decides another. Six defects that need no ruling are listed first, each with a Monday owner. That is an owner's document in a way page A never attempts.

Three things stop it from being enough on its own.

- The *rests on* column reads "both" on eight rows where the card voids the buyer leg. An owner ruling from the sheet will over-weight his own sales record on exactly the cards where it proves least.
- Eleven of the 24 rows say "commission the observation, or rule between the two", and six of those eleven need a season with two shapes on sale or a fresh market survey. Those are budget decisions with a lead time, not rulings, and they sit in the same table and the same typeface as "confirm".
- The margin column mixes units without warning: "14 units", "5 slots", "480 a month", "flat with no extras rate 5 of 16". Card 20's "5 slots" is one web page.

Add the eleven-withheld paragraphs and the collision notes and it is 5,013 words, about twenty-five minutes, which is the honest figure for a first sitting. He opens a card only where a row raises a doubt, and on this set the rows that should raise one are 2, 10, 12, 18 and 20.

## Does page B withhold too much or too little?

**Of the 11 withheld cards, 11 are sound and 0 are evasive.** Every one names the specific thing that would carry it, and on 8 of the 11 the reason is that the leg which leads was measured in a population this venue is not in, which is a real disqualifier and not a dodge. The one a marketer will argue with is card 21: promoting "publishes nothing" to a rival option on a social-proof card converts an uncontested market gap into a case for doing nothing. It still prints the 9-to-4 lead among the sixteen operators that state a posture, so it does not conceal the evidence. It declines to rank silence against speech, which is defensible and also, commercially, the wrong instinct. Page A recommended publishing testimonials and was right to.

**Of the 13 cards that recommend, three should have withheld.**

1. **Card 2**, on the denominator objection above. It is the page's one UNSUPPORTED row and it sits third in the order of ruling, deciding the composition of the fee.
2. **Card 20**, on n=1. "the lead is a count of one page and would be erased by a different head term, so the second capture named on the measured-in line is what would firm it" is the description of a withheld card, written on a recommending one.
3. **Card 12**, more arguably. It recommends putting a paid body on every party morning, at E29's \$150 a shift against a pathway with no coordinator rostered, on a 5-unit publication lead that the card itself says three operators coded the other way would reverse, with no buyer anywhere in the record asking for a host. The farm-style cell (10 of 17) is what saves it from the card-2 objection; the cost line is what should have stopped it short of a recommendation.

**Card 10 is the one to watch rather than to reclassify.** The count carries it, 25 of 45 at three hours or less against 2 above, but the two pages read the same fact in opposite directions, and the owner should see that. Page A treats four hours' exclusive use as the venue's unmatched advantage and prices *upward* on it: "F71 (four hours' exclusive use has no like-for-like counterpart at any price in the catchment)." Page B treats the same fact as being out of step and recommends cutting to three. Both cannot be right. Page B's own card names the single closest-shaped precedent, Collingwood, at four hours, and rules past it.

## The three changes that would most improve page B for one sitting

1. **Fix the *rests on* column.** Where a card's condition line says the venue's record carries nothing, the sheet must not say "both". Set those eight rows to "market" and give the own-record material its own column headed something like *what our own buyers can and cannot show*, because on this set the honest entry for most rows is "nothing: only one shape was ever on sale". That single change is what converts the page's best reasoning from something buried in the cards into something visible on the sheet.
2. **Split the sheet in two.** Thirteen rows are rulings he can make on Monday. Eleven are commissions, and six of those need a season or a fresh survey before anybody can rule. Print them as two tables, *decide now* and *what to buy before you can decide*, with a cost and an owner against each commission, as the six "wrong today" items already carry. As it stands the sheet asks him to read eleven rows he cannot act on in the same pass as thirteen he can.
3. **Recount card 2 on the matched population, and mark card 20's denominator on the sheet.** Card 2 should be re-run the way cards 4, 11 and 22 are, over operators selling a party into a site that stays open free around it, and should say what it finds even if the finding is that the count cannot rank it. Card 20's margin cell should read "7 of 9 slots, one capture, one term, one date" rather than "5 slots".

## LEDGER

**Part 1: work the review's goal needs that this brief does not cover.**

- The brief asks for a verdict on each card but not for a verdict on the *order of ruling*, which is where most of the owner's risk sits. Card 12 silently decides card 2, card 15 decides card 6, card 17 changes cards 16 and 18, and card 11 strips card 7's strongest item. Nobody has checked whether the stated order actually resolves those dependencies without a cycle. That check should exist before the order is used.
- No judge has been asked whether the eleven commissions are affordable or sequenceable. Six need a season with two shapes on sale, which on a weekend-only party calendar of roughly four slots a week is a year of trading. If they cannot all be bought, the page needs a priority among them, and nothing in either page gives one.
- Page A's four named gaps (instant confirmation, the do-it-yourself alternative's cost, the booking-route distribution, duration comparability) were routed back to Survey. Only the first appears in page B's "wrong today" list. Whether the other three were answered, dropped or forgotten between 15 August and 20 September is not recorded on either page, and the duration gap is the one that feeds card 10, the card this review flags as contested.
- The judgement on whether the method carries to six further runs needs a cost figure the brief does not ask for: page B's 24 cards, three card clerks, a priors clerk, a third-derivation clerk and a two-pass corrections clerk produced 31,561 words plus 24 third-derivation files and a card file each. Six runs at that scale is a capacity decision, and no one has stated what a run costs.
