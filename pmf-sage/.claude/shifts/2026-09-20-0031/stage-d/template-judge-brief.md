# Brief templates before they reach another run: a review for the owner

A product-research method (its documents are `sage-methodology.md`, `sage-S-survey.md`, `sage-A-adjudicate.md` and the forms under `forms/`, at `/usr/local/src/aesop/pmf-sage/`) runs as a line of clerks, each a fresh reader given one brief. The brief templates sit in `/usr/local/src/aesop/pmf-sage/.claude/shifts/2026-09-19-1042/stage-d/briefs/`. They were rewritten on 20 September 2026 from the lessons of two runs (`../batch-two-lessons-by-clerk.md` beside that folder, and `../pair-lessons-by-clerk.md` from the two runs before). They are about to be filled in for two further offerings (the on-sale state of each run is in its standing block), each with a corpus of several hundred operators whose pages were kept as text.

You review the templates for the owner before any clerk reads them. He has two worries, in his words. The first: work that "predicts what market needs by meeting notes and owner inner thoughts instead of reality". The second, its mirror: "thinking if owner believe A, A must be incorrect". A sentence in a brief can fail either way, and a sentence that fails is read by every clerk who gets that brief, in every run after this one.

Read every template whole, and the two lessons files. Open the method's documents where you need to check what a template claims the method says. Then answer, quoting the template's words each time and naming the file:

1. Tilt. Every sentence that would move a clerk toward what the venue already holds, or away from it, whatever the evidence shows. Include a sentence that is fair as written and will be read unfairly by a clerk in a hurry.
2. Hardening. Every sentence that states as fixed something no one with authority has ruled: a parameter of an offering, a design of the programme, a threshold that one run happened to use. Say who would have to rule it.
3. Carry-over. Every sentence that fits the two runs it was learnt on and will misfire on an offering never sold: what "the held value", "the sales record", "what is on sale" or a named count can mean where nothing has been sold; nouns of the earlier runs' trades left in a template.
4. A rule as prose. The lessons say a rule that several clerks apply alike is a file, a table or a script and not a sentence. Name every place the templates themselves hand several clerks a counting or grouping rule as a sentence.
5. Contradiction. Any two templates, or a template and the method's documents, that tell a clerk opposite things. Quote both.
6. Weight. Any passage that tells a capable reader what it already knows, or narrates the earlier runs, where the instruction would stand without it. Give the word count that could go.

Close with one line for each template: READY, MEND (with the replacement sentence you would write) or HOLD (it should not reach a run as it stands, and why). Then the three mends that matter most. Judge the templates as they are; whether the lessons behind them were the right lessons is a separate question, and if you think one was the wrong lesson, say so in a short last section and say what evidence would show it.

Write the full review to the output file your prompt names, and report its path with the per-template line and the three mends in under 250 words. Edit no template.
