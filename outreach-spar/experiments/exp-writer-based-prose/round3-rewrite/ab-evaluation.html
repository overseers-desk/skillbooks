Outreach letters, round 4, 23 September 2026

Does writing it again help?

Two ways of asking the author of a cold letter to write it a second time, for the reader rather than from the brief. On the model that wrote the letters, neither improved them: the sentences that belong to the writer stayed, the references the reader cannot resolve stayed, and the letters grew. On the newer model, a small check shows one design gaining, and the gain cannot be told apart from the model change.

Design A, one call

Draft, then write again

no improvement

The author drafts the letter and, in the same call, reads it as the recipient would and writes it again. Eight contacts, one of them phone-only with no letter, so seven pairs: first draft against final, measures defined under Measured.

Design B, second prompt

Finished letter, then asked again

no improvement

The session that wrote a finished letter is prompted once more to write it again for its reader. Twenty-two letters, nineteen written before a usage limit stopped it: sent letter against second writing.

The question

The campaign offers local organisations free tickets to the opening week of a circus season in a big top at a riverside venue, with a code and a booking link sent on reply. The letters were drafted from that campaign's brief in the office's outreach method, in two earlier rounds that tested guards around the drafting loop, and a third that had a second writer, holding only the letter, write it again. A letter drafted from a campaign brief tends to carry the brief into the reader's inbox: why the offer exists, how the reader came to be written to, what is not on offer, references to a code and a link the reader has never met. This page calls it the Linda Flower problem, after her account of writer-based prose. Whether those sentences are true is a separate matter: two of the brief's claims were false at source and have since been cut from it, and this page measures where the sentences come from and whether the reader can follow them, not their truth. Her remedy is that the writer, holding everything they know, steps back and writes again to the reader's questions. The round before this one tested a different thing by mistake: a second writer who held only the letter. This round tests the remedy as she states it, twice.

Design A. One author call. The instruction appended to the ordinary drafting brief:

Two drafts in the output. Write a first draft of the message between
FIRST_START and FIRST_END lines. Then read it as the recipient would,
and write the final message to what they would ask, between DRAFT_START
and DRAFT_END. The rationale keeps its markers. The message between
DRAFT_START and DRAFT_END is the one that goes out.

Design B. The author session that produced a finished, challenged, sent-ready letter is resumed and given one prompt:

Write the letter again for its reader, who has seen none of what you
read in order to write it. Write to what they would ask. Output the
subject line first as "Subject: ...", then a blank line, then the body,
and nothing else.

In both, the writer holds its inputs: the plan block, the registry, the recipient's profile, the fact sources, and in B every round of the challenger's feedback. Nothing is hidden from it. What changes is only that it is asked to write to the reader.

Measured

All letters were shuffled under random names and scored in one pass by one reader that never saw which was which, under a written rubric applied unchanged: references that resolve nowhere, sentences a reader would wonder why they are being told, scored 0 to 5 and counted at 4 or 5, and sentences on the sender's side of the matter rather than the reader's: why the offer is made, how the recipient came to be written to, what is not on offer, the sender's situation. The reader also counted false and unsupported claims; those belong to the brief's truth, not its organisation, and sit in the repository rather than here. Each second writing is paired with its own first. A second run of the same reader over the same letters, started by accident, agrees with the first on the direction of every measure; the two correlate at 0.95 on sender-side sentences and 0.99 on words, and between 0.64 and 0.77 on the other counts. What the second writing did

It kept every sentence the brief supplied and reordered the rest. The sentences that explain the offer from the sender's side, why it exists, how the recipient came to be written to, what is not on offer, are the brief's own reasoning, and the author holds the brief as the offer's statement. Told to write again for the reader, it writes them again, in a better order.

One number moved the right way: design A's unsupported claims fell, in two of seven pairs. Everything else held or worsened.

The other thing it did was add. Design B's second writings run about forty words longer than the letters they replace. Design A's finals run about thirty longer than their firsts, and the additions are details the first draft had not needed: a show's running time, a street address, a second channel the brief mentioned. Read side by side, the final draft is the first draft with more of the brief in it.

The same two designs on Opus 5.5, five pairs each

A smaller check on the newer model, scored in its own pass by the same reader. Design A, both drafts by Opus 5.5, moves the same way as on Opus 5: longer by about forty words, worse on unresolved references. Design B moves the other way: Opus 5.5's second writing of a letter that Opus 5 wrote cut unresolved references in three of five pairs and surprising sentences in four, and added only seven words.

What the B result on 5.5 cannot say is whether the gain is the second pass or the newer model rewriting an older model's letter. The letters B starts from were all written by Opus 5, so a B pair on 5.5 changes model as well as pass. Design A, where both passes are 5.5, did not improve. The reading that fits both is that Opus 5.5 writes a tighter letter than Opus 5 from the same brief, and asking it to write again does not add to that. A B pair whose first writing is also 5.5 would settle it and needs the harness run on 5.5.

Why

The writer does not know which of its sentences are the reader's and which are its own. The sentences that come from the brief are, to it, the offer itself. Asked to write for the reader, it writes them for the reader, more carefully and at more length. The instruction reaches the surface of the prose, its order and its address, and not the selection of what is in it. The cut has to come from a check that holds the brief and asks a different question of each sentence: is this here because an input said so, or because the reader would ask. That is the challenger's fact-check with its question changed, not a second pass by the author.

What is not settled

  • Size. Seven pairs for A and nineteen for B separate a direction, not an effect size. The direction is the same in every measure and both designs.
  • Model. The letters B rewrote were written by Opus 5. The alias opus had moved to Opus 5.5 by the morning of the run, so the first B run and the first A run changed model as well as pass. Both were rerun on Opus 5 by its exact id; those are the main numbers. Five pairs of each design from the Opus 5.5 runs are the smaller check above.
  • Resume appends. Prompting a session a second time leaves the first rewrite in its context. The B letters here come from a copy of each session cut before any rewrite prompt, so the writer had only its original drafting in view.
  • Three B letters missing. The Claude subscription profile those sessions live on reached its weekly usage limit during the run; it resets on 24 September at 7pm.
  • A first wording of A was refused. Asked to write the message "as it comes" under a working marker, Opus refused all eight calls as reasoning extraction. Probes showed the wording was the trigger, not the model's context or the prefetched correspondence block in the brief. Reworded as two drafts, all eight passed.
