Outreach letters, round 4, 23 September 2026

Does writing it again help?

Two ways of asking the author of a cold letter to write it a second time, for the reader rather than from the brief. Neither improved the letter. Both made it longer, and the claims that were wrong in the first writing were wrong in the second.

Design A, one call

Draft, then write again

no improvement

The author drafts the letter and, in the same call, reads it as the recipient would and writes it again. Seven contacts, first draft against final.

Design B, second prompt

Finished letter, then asked again

no improvement

The session that wrote a finished letter is prompted once more to write it again for its reader. Nineteen contacts, sent letter against second writing.

The question

A letter drafted from a campaign brief tends to carry the brief into the reader's inbox: why the offer exists, how the reader was picked, what is not on sale, references to a code and a link the reader has never met. The office calls this the Linda Flower problem, after her account of writer-based prose. Her remedy is that the writer, holding everything they know, steps back and writes again to the reader's questions. The round before this one tested a different thing by mistake: a second writer who held only the letter. This round tests the remedy as she states it, twice.

Design A. One author call. The instruction appended to the ordinary drafting brief:

Two drafts in the output. Write a first draft of the message between
FIRST_START and FIRST_END lines. Then read it as the recipient would,
and write the final message to what they would ask, between DRAFT_START
and DRAFT_END. The rationale keeps its markers. The message between
DRAFT_START and DRAFT_END is the one that goes out.

Design B. The author session that produced a finished, challenged, sent-ready letter is resumed and given one prompt:

Write the letter again for its reader, who has seen none of what you
read in order to write it. Write to what they would ask. Output the
subject line first as "Subject: ...", then a blank line, then the body,
and nothing else.

In both, the writer holds its inputs: the campaign plan, the claims registry, the recipient's profile, the fact sources, and in B every round of the challenger's feedback. Nothing is hidden from it. What changes is only that it is asked to write to the reader.

Measured

All letters were shuffled under random names and scored in one pass by one reader that never saw which was which, under a written rubric applied unchanged: references that resolve nowhere, sentences a reader would wonder why they are being told, claims contradicted by the venue's facts, and sentences on the sender's side of the matter rather than the reader's. Each second writing is paired with its own first.

What the second writing did

It kept every claim the brief supplied and reordered the rest. The two claims known to be false, that the seats are not on sale and that the recipient was picked, come from the campaign's registry, which the author treats as the offer's own statement. A writer told to write again for the reader re-reads that registry and writes them again. Counted by phrase over the paired letters:

The other thing it did was add. Design B's second writings run about forty words longer than the letters they replace. Design A's finals run about thirty longer than their firsts, and the additions are details the first draft had not needed: a show's running time, a street address, a second channel the brief mentioned. Read side by side, the final draft is the first draft with more of the brief in it.

Why

The writer does not know which of its sentences are the reader's and which are its own. The sentences that come from the brief are, to it, the facts of the offer. Asked to write for the reader, it writes those facts for the reader, more carefully and at more length. The instruction reaches the surface of the prose, its order and its address, and not the selection of what is in it. The cut has to come from a check that holds the brief and asks a different question of each sentence: is this here because an input said so, or because the reader would ask. That is the challenger's fact-check with its question changed, not a second pass by the author.

What is not settled

  • Size. Seven pairs for A and nineteen for B separate a direction, not an effect size. The direction is the same in every measure and both designs.
  • Model. The letters B rewrote were written by Opus 5. The alias opus had moved to Opus 5.5 by the morning of the run, so the first B run and the first A run changed model as well as pass. Both were rerun on Opus 5 by its exact id; those are the numbers here. The Opus 5.5 material is kept in the folder and not scored.
  • Resume appends. Prompting a session a second time leaves the first rewrite in its context. The B letters here come from a copy of each session cut before any rewrite prompt, so the writer had only its original drafting in view.
  • Three B letters missing. The profile those sessions live on reached its weekly usage limit during the run; it resets on 24 September at 7pm.
  • A first wording of A was refused. Asked to write the message "as it comes" under a working marker, Opus refused all eight calls as reasoning extraction. Probes showed the wording was the trigger, not the model's context or the courier block. Reworded as two drafts, all eight passed.
