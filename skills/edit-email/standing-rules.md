# standing-rules

Four rules that hold for every email this skill touches, alongside the numbered rules in `email-rulebook.md`. Each has a short name the rulebook and the caller use as a cue; the name means what is written here and nothing else.

## Newspaper test

An email is read the way a newspaper reader reads an article in his own trade: he knows the industry's standard terms and nothing of the sender's project. Industry jargon stays. A term the sender's project coined, or that the drafting agent coined while working, is translated into plain language where it is used: the sentence says what the term means, in the reader's words, and the term goes. Do not keep the term and add a definition beside it, and do not paste in the document the term came from; either way the reader has to learn the project's vocabulary to read the mail. The editor's move is to cut the project-specific term and put its meaning in its place.

## SDT: show, don't tell

An email does its work through its substance. One that narrates its own purpose cancels the move: a poem written to win someone over, captioned "The poem written to win you over", wins nobody. So the mail does not say what it is for, what it is not about, or how the reader should take it ("just a friendly note", "this is not a complaint", "I'm writing to reassure you"). It is friendly, or reassuring, or not a complaint, by what it says. The same holds for a reframe: asked to present a setback as an opportunity, write in the new frame; "this is not a setback but an opportunity" corrects a framing the reader never had.

## NSWP: no solution without problem

Every sentence answers a question the recipient has, or will have once he reads the ask. Two patterns fail the test. A sentence that solves a problem nobody raised: a reassurance against a worry the reader never had, an option offered for a situation that has not arisen, a fallback for a fault that is not there. Asked why such a sentence is there, a writer invents the problem from the absence of the solution; that invention is the tell. And a sentence written for the writer rather than the reader: an account that starts from how the sender's side works, or how the draft came to be, before it reaches the reader's own problem. Name the reader's problem before the cure; where the sentence cannot name one, cut it.

## Densify

To densify is to remove connective tissue and hedging while keeping every concrete specific and every epistemic marker. Specifics are dates, numbers, magnitudes absolute and relative, named entities, and actors named by role. Epistemic markers say how a fact is known: checked and found empty, unobtainable, not attempted, approximated, inferred. Replacing a specific with a generic or a qualitative word ("recently" for a date, "a few" for a count) is summarising, not densifying: it saves little and loses what the reader acts on. Collapsing a marker loses the difference between absence of evidence and evidence of absence: "the search timed out, so no earlier mail was found" is not "there was no earlier mail". When the user or a rule asks for a shorter email, densify rather than summarise, since what summarising drops is what the recipient acts on.
