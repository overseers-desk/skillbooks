# Liansu style guide

This guide is for subeditors polishing email drafts written on behalf of Liansu Yu, owner and director of Historic Rivermill. It sits on top of the standard R-rules and covers only what is specific to her voice.

A note on corpus scope: many kept files contain emails written by staff (Bárbara, Vikki, Priyanka, Weiwu) rather than Liansu directly. Observations here are drawn only from emails signed as Liansu or authored by her account (my@colourful.land, yuliansu@gmail.com) with her name at the close.

---

## 1. Formality level

Liansu's register sits consistently in the middle: neither corporate-formal nor casual-conversational. She is warmer than a lawyer's letter but more collected than a text message.

**With business contacts and external partners** (suppliers, accountants, insurance, legal, wedding planners, journalists): clear, professional, cordial. She uses full sentences, real subject lines, and proper sign-offs. She does not mirror the formality of the counterpart upward — if a bank writes to her with heavy formality, she replies in her own measured register, not theirs. Example: replying to Judo Bank with "Just reaching out about the loan repayment that didn't go through" (0010) rather than matching their legal phrasing.

**With staff and regular collaborators** (Mike, Priyanka, John, Belinda): marginally more direct, sometimes drops the opening pleasantry, uses first-name address, occasionally sends one-liners. "Signed, thanks! LS" (0041). "Hi team, I want to inform you..." (0173). But she never drops professional standards even with close colleagues.

**With clients and customers** (wedding enquiries, event bookings, stall vendors): consistently warm, welcoming, and service-oriented. More likely to use "we're excited" or "looking forward to a wonderful celebration" language.

**With the school (teachers, admissions)** (emails in Spanish/English to Altillo school staff): her most formally structured writing. Clear paragraph logic, explicit apology where inconvenience is caused, detailed explanation of circumstances. In Spanish she uses "Estimada/Estimado" and closes "Atentamente." In English she mirrors the same structure.

**The variation rule:** formality shifts by recipient category, not by topic. A message about a complex financial matter to a close collaborator is still informal; a short message to an external school administrator is formal.

## 2. Sentence structure

Sentences are short to medium. She rarely uses subordinate clauses stacked three-deep. Most sentences are one to two clauses. She is not afraid of short standalone sentences.

She favours a pattern of: one sentence stating the situation, one sentence making the ask or next step. "I noticed the invoice has been made out for 8 trestle tables, but our order is actually for 6. It looks like there may have been a small mix-up at the quoting stage." (0049).

She uses run-on sentences occasionally when hurrying through a list of thoughts, particularly in internal emails. "Please let us know the next steps and send over the courier details so we can coordinate the pickup." (0009). These are natural, not sloppy — they convey momentum.

She avoids heavy nominalisations. "I wanted to check if you might be available" (0093) rather than "I am seeking your availability." She writes close to how she would speak.

She uses the dash (not em-dash) for an aside or a soft correction: "I noticed a few Acuity bookings still coming through, and I wasn't entirely sure of their source" uses no dash, but she uses brackets similarly: "(does it resolve yet)" (0256), "(when you have a chance, can you share a photo? thanks)" (0256).

## 3. Vocabulary and phrasing

**Frequent phrases and words:**
- "kindly" — her default polite intensifier for requests. "Kindly ensure," "kindly reply-all," "kindly cc my personal email." Used 10+ times across the corpus. This is a signature word.
- "Cheers" — her most frequent informal sign-off. Used even with external suppliers and event partners.
- "Regards" — her neutral sign-off; less warm than "Cheers," used with newer contacts or when the tone is more transactional.
- "Thanks" and "Thank you" appear in virtually every email, usually at the open or close.
- "I hope you're doing well" / "I hope this email finds you well" — she uses these genuine pleasantries with contacts she has an ongoing relationship with, not cold contacts.
- "I'd love to get your thoughts" / "looking forward to hearing your thoughts" — she uses "thoughts" where a more formal writer would use "feedback" or "input."
- "We're excited" / "We can't wait" — appears in client-facing emails for events and weddings. Genuine enthusiasm about the venue and events.
- "Quick recap" / "Quick note" / "Quick update" — she flags short internal messages this way, not as a stylistic tic but as a genuine signal of brevity.
- "In light of this" — used as a pivot before a request or consequence. Not overused.
- "Happy to" — preferred to "I am able to" or "I can." "Happy to set up time to walk through your checklist" (0203), "Happy to talk it through" (0181).
- "I wanted to" — her natural way to introduce a purpose. "I wanted to share," "I wanted to check," "I wanted to inform you." She does not say "I am writing to."

**Words she does not use:**
- She does not write "please be advised," "please find enclosed," "as per," "in reference to your," "please do not hesitate to contact me" (this last one appears in emails from staff, not from her).
- She does not write "I hope this email finds you well" to cold contacts or people she has never met.
- She avoids the corporate passive: "it was decided," "it has been noted." She uses active first-person.
- She does not use "ensure" in the manner of a checklist instruction. That word appears in staff-authored emails, not hers.
- She almost never uses "whilst" (one instance appears in a more formal English draft, possibly influenced by a prior draft).

**Numbers and specifics:** she is comfortable embedding concrete numbers naturally in a sentence. "47 guests on Saturday, August 23, at 12:00 p.m." (0012). She doesn't hedge numbers with approximate language unless the number is genuinely approximate ("about two hours" [0250], "~40 guests" [0071]).

## 4. Email openings

**Salutation patterns:**
- "Hi [Name]," — most common with known contacts. Friendly.
- "Hello [Name]," — slightly more formal, used with external contacts she doesn't know well, or for multiple recipients. "Hello Rino & Fiorella," (0009).
- "Hello team," / "Hello Everyone," — for group/staff emails.
- "Dear [Name/Title]," — reserved for formal contexts: school teachers, legal correspondence, Spanish-language formal emails. "Dear Mrs. Warford," (0052), "Estimada equipo de admisiones," (0291).
- No salutation — very short internal replies, or functional one-liners: "Signed, thanks! LS" (0041). Rare.

She does not use "Good morning/afternoon/evening" as a salutation in her own voice (this appears in Vikki's and Bárbara's emails, not Liansu's).

**First-sentence patterns:**
She does not put the main ask in the first sentence. She almost always opens with a context-setter or a pleasantry:
- A pleasantry: "I hope you're doing well." / "Thanks for visiting Rivermill on Wednesday!" (0251)
- A brief acknowledgement of the prior contact: "Thanks for choosing Historic Rivermill for your special day." (0012), "Thanks for your message, we're excited for your son's special day!" (0140)
- A brief statement of why she's writing: "I wanted to share the Palacio calendar with you." (0084), "I'd like to share our current venue tour bookings." (0215)
- An apology where relevant: "Thank you for your email, and apologies for the delayed reply." (0168), "Thank you so much for your email, and my sincere apologies for the delayed response." (0256)

She opens with the recipient's perspective, not her own agenda. When she starts with "I," it is in the context of a benefit being offered or context being given, not a demand.

## 5. Email closings

**Sign-off patterns:**

- "Cheers, Liansu" — most common, across a wide range of contexts including external business contacts. Distinctively Australian-informal for someone running a formal venue. She uses it with suppliers, event planners, accountants.
- "Cheers, Liansu xx" — adds "xx" with people she has warm rapport with: Belinda (celebrant), Sacha, John (colleagues). "Regards, Liansu xx" (0228). The "xx" is a warmth marker, not a romantic one.
- "Kind regards, Liansu" — with more formal external contacts: Judo Bank, lawyers, insurance. Less common than "Cheers" across the corpus.
- "Best regards" — appears occasionally, similar contexts to "Kind regards."
- "Regards, Liansu" — brief and neutral, often in internal operational emails.
- "LS" — when very short: "Regards, LS" (0234), "Signed, thanks! LS" (0041).
- "Best, Liansu" — informal without being as casual as "Cheers."

She almost never closes with full title in the sign-off body itself; her signature block ("Owner | Historic Rivermill®") carries the title. She signs with just her first name.

## 6. How she makes requests

Her requests are direct but softened. She does not apologise for making a request, but she tends to frame it with a rationale or context before stating it, and she tends to give the recipient room to manoeuvre.

**Softening devices she uses:**
- "Could you please" — very common. "Could you please arrange for your courier to pick up all 123 bottles?" (0009). Not "Please arrange." Not "I need you to."
- "Would you be able to" — for requests where she's genuinely uncertain of the person's capacity. "Would you be able to put together a quick cost breakdown per head?" (0020).
- "I'd love to get your thoughts" (0084) — makes a request sound like an invitation.
- "If you're interested and available" (0005) — leaves full room to decline.
- "Kindly" — used as a softener rather than as a formality marker. It appears in both formal and informal contexts.
- She explains why before asking. In the catering cost request (0020) she provides three paragraphs of context (margin calculation, cost structure, deadline) before making the ask. In the horse helper rate email (0060) she explains the problem before asking Priyanka for the report.

**When she is escalating urgency:** she becomes more direct and removes softeners. "I need an urgent update on the status of our connected bank details. We have emailed multiple times with no response, and this lack of communication is now causing operational delays on our end. Please confirm immediately." (0036). Even here, she avoids personal criticism of the recipient; she describes the impact on operations, not their failure.

**When she is declining or setting limits:** she is polite but clear. "I'm afraid I'm not able to sign a Statement of Service with a different official title or responsibilities than what was formally held." (0168). She explains the principle without lengthy justification.

She does not write lists of action items for the recipient. If she needs multiple things, she groups them within prose or uses a simple sequential structure ("First..." / "Additionally...") rather than a numbered task list aimed at the reader.

## 7. Paragraph structure

Her paragraphs are short: typically two to four sentences. She does not write multi-sentence topic sentences followed by multiple supporting sentences; each paragraph carries one thought and then stops.

She tends to build in this pattern:
1. One paragraph of context or acknowledgement.
2. One paragraph of the main point or ask.
3. One paragraph of next steps or closing warmth.

She does not summarise what she has just said. She ends on the forward-looking point or the warmth, not on a restatement.

In longer emails (meeting summaries, operational briefs), she uses section headers to structure the content rather than mega-paragraphs. She bullet-points factual lists (times, prices, attendee lists) when listing parallel items, but does not use bullets for prose content.

She uses "@" mentions within email to direct an action to a specific team member when the email has multiple recipients. "@Michael Wood <manager@rivermill.au> can you confirm that we will go ahead with purchasing the 15 bottles?" (0204). This is her way of keeping a group email and a direct ask in one message.

## 8. Warmth and tone

Liansu's warmth is genuine and specific. She does not apply generic pleasantries uniformly; warmth appears more in emails where she has an ongoing relationship or where she is welcoming a client's special occasion.

**When warmth is high:**
- With clients celebrating weddings, birthdays, family events: "We can't wait to help celebrate your wife's funtastic 40th birthday!" (0012). Note the non-standard word "funtastic" — she echoes the customer's word choice back to them.
- With suppliers she has built rapport with: "they are amazing humans" (0009) — about Fiorella and Rino of Pucara Pisco, written in a three-way introduction email.
- With staff she trusts: "Thank you xx" (0089) to graphic designer Tsiko. "Thanks so much — really appreciate you jumping on this." (0020).
- With the school community around her children: personal detail shared naturally — "Alice didn't sleep well last night — I think the time change (daylight saving) and the upcoming trip have affected her a bit." (0147).

**When warmth is lower but still present:**
- Financial and legal matters with institutions: tone is polite but businesslike. No "looking forward to" warmth. She is courteous but focused.
- Correction or escalation: she stays firm without becoming cold. "This is a habit worth dropping, especially externally." (0181) — direct but not hostile.

**What she doesn't do:**
She does not project excitement or enthusiasm she doesn't feel. She does not close a debt-chasing email with "Looking forward to hearing from you!" — that would be out of register. Her warmth is proportional to the relationship and occasion.

## 9. What would betray a non-author

These constructions or patterns would immediately read as not-Liansu:

**Wrong salutations:** "Good morning/afternoon [Name]" — this belongs to Vikki's register, not Liansu's. Liansu says "Hi" or "Hello."

**"Please do not hesitate to contact me"** — this is Vikki's phrase. Liansu does not use it.

**"Please find attached/enclosed"** — she writes "I'm sharing," "I've attached," or just "attached is." The passive-administrative construction is absent from her voice.

**"As per my previous email" or "as per our discussion"** — she recaps directly: "As discussed in our meeting, I am sharing the process." She doesn't use "as per."

**"I am writing to inform you"** — she doesn't use this construction. She writes "I wanted to let you know" or simply starts with the information.

**Lists of numbered action items addressed to the recipient** — she structures asks within prose or uses soft sequencing ("First... Additionally..."). A bulleted list of "please do items 1-5" is staff style (Priyanka, Bárbara), not Liansu's.

**Generic closing warmth regardless of context** — "Looking forward to your response!" attached to a debt-chasing or complaint email would not appear from Liansu. Her closings match the emotional register of the content.

**"Ensure that" or "Please ensure"** — she says "please make sure" or "I'd appreciate it if you could..." The word "ensure" as a standalone imperative is Priyanka/admin register.

**"I hope this email finds you well"** as a cold-contact opener — she uses this only with contacts she has an ongoing relationship with. To a first-time contact she opens differently.

**Over-long rationale chains** — in short operational emails she is concise. If a reply is naturally a one-liner ("I confirm both 1st September 2026 or 2027 are available" [0071]) she sends a one-liner. She does not pad short answers.

**The AI hedge-chain**: "I wanted to reach out to you to let you know that..." — she would write "I wanted to let you know that..." or simply state the fact. She doesn't add extra motion-verbs before getting to the point.
